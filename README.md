# Wonderloop

Make it. Prove it. Say it.

Wonderloop is a home curriculum for children, packaged as a small web app one family runs for
itself. It exists because a bright child at home has an hour or two a week that school does not
use, and because the things that make that hour count (making something real, explaining it,
teaching it to someone younger, coming back to an idea weeks later) are easy to describe and
hard to keep doing without a structure. Wonderloop is that structure: a twelve-week season at a
time, one week at a time, with a real product at the end of every week.

It is opinionated. It is built for a numerically strong child who is bored by grade-level pacing
and for a toddler sibling who is not yet in school, and it says so rather than pretending to be
for everyone. It is also honest: nothing is "done" without a checked answer, a photo, a
recording or a written log, and there are no coins, streaks or daily guilt anywhere in it.

## Who it is for

- **An Explorer**, roughly ages 8 to 11. Programming, robotics and AI; math and logic well ahead
  of grade; public speaking, debate and a little philosophy; and, if they take music lessons,
  instrument practice that actually happens.
- **A Sprout**, roughly ages 2 to 4. Pre-math and pre-coding foundations (patterns, sorting,
  sequencing, counting, shapes) as short tap-and-listen activities, plus one hands-on card a
  week for a grown-up to run.
- **A parent**, about an hour a week: the Showcase, the Sprout card, reading the logs, the
  occasional thumbs-up, and the shopping list.

Several families can run the same app, each in its own household with its own children, its
own week clock and its own data. One household never sees another.

## The shape of a week

An Explorer gets four or five quests a week, each about an hour, and the week's card shows all
of them at once.

- **Build** (60 minutes): a real thing to make with a micro:bit, a breadboard, a small robot, or
  Python, with the engineering loop named out loud (ask, imagine, plan, build, test, improve),
  a science moment, a data step where the week has real numbers, a bug diary, and a Maker's Log
  at the end.
- **Make** (60 minutes, from season 2): engineering toward robotics, three seasons of it. Levers, gears and torque
  measured with a spring scale; parts measured with calipers, modelled and 3D printed for the
  robot; structures loaded to failure; a circuit drawn as a schematic and measured against its
  datasheet; motors and their drivers; the robot upgraded with all of it, proven before and after.
  Then a battery's capacity measured, a sensor board designed in KiCad and fabricated, water
  rockets, orbit as falling and missing, a servo pan-tilt; and an ESP32, a radio link, a Mars rover
  brief bound by light time, thermal and solar measurements, an autonomous mission, transistors to
  gates to real logic chips, the rover proven. Every week has a data step: the child's own numbers in a table, charted, with one claim
  answered from them.
- **Think** (90 minutes): a ten-minute warm-up of number sense (or a Fermi estimate, or rhythm
  as fractions), a spatial puzzle of the week, a skills lane that stays ahead of grade, a
  geometry-and-chance lane (compass and straightedge, angles, similarity, coordinates, and
  probability from real dice), and a puzzle-and-proof lane that ends in a written "explain it". Every problem ships with two
  authored hints that ask rather than tell, a Socratic question for the third try, and its own
  explanation, so nothing depends on an AI being up.
- **Speak** (45 minutes): a talk, or from week 5 a three-round debate on the week's philosophy
  question, recorded for the portfolio. Once a sprint the Explorer runs the Sprout card for
  someone younger; teaching it is the proof of knowing it.
- **Play** (60 minutes, optional): instrument practice for a child who takes lessons, as four
  short sittings built around one practice trick a week (hard bars first, slow is smooth,
  hands alone then together, loop the join, listen back, the last line first, the map of the
  piece), an old piece played again every week, a recording with the hands in frame, and a
  Practice log, across all four seasons. A parent turns it on per child; the other tracks do not
  change.

A season is twelve weeks: three sprints of three quest weeks, each closed by a **Showcase**
week where the Explorer presents to the family, debates a parent, and (with Play on) gives a
mini recital. A monster problem sits on the side each month with no time limit. One book per
season, from a curated list, is discussed at the last Showcase.

A Sprout gets three activities of about five minutes, with spoken prompts, big tap targets and
no fail state, each ending by sending the child off-screen ("go find three red things"), and the
parent card. Screen time in the app is capped at fifteen minutes a day.

## The four seasons

Each season has a terrain and an arc, and every quest in it is authored in full: 156 quests,
874 problems, 48 Sprout weeks.

1. **Forest.** A name badge on a micro:bit in week 1; a breadboard LED and button; a robot that
   drives a square, follows a line and avoids obstacles; a machine taught to recognise hand
   signs; first Python. Fractions, decimals, ratios and unit price; knights and knaves, parity,
   pigeonhole, first written proofs, Bebras sets. The 60-second show-and-tell, the steelman
   rule, "what makes something fair?".
2. **Coast.** Logic gates in copper, binary on one hand, a machine that adds; soldering; a robot
   with moods; a free build pitched, planned, priced and built for a real person. Exponents,
   Nim and winning positions, counting and probability, primes and remainders, angles and area.
   Claims you can check, the skeptical judge.
3. **Mountains.** Python on the laptop, loops over data, code at an address you can give away;
   a small shop run for real, with the money in two pockets and a machine that tries every
   price; the first competition-programming stories with the real grader. Negatives, the
   mystery number in both pans, percent in the shop's mirror. Formal debate: the format, the
   timer, the ballot, and the side you are handed.
4. **Desert.** A second programming language beside the first; reading every story before
   writing; the card you write while you are calm; writing and grading a contest problem of
   your own. Slope, roots, the bridges of Konigsberg, the smallest criminal. The judge's chair
   and the sealed envelope. The arc closes with a dated map of the real peaks ahead.

## How it accelerates learning

The curriculum is the product; the app is its container. What the app adds is the discipline
the curriculum needs and a family cannot keep by hand.

- **Named ideas, met and retrieved.** Every problem carries the idea it teaches. The idea box
  shows which ideas a child has met; a wrong first try comes back weeks later in a new shape
  through the mistake box; science steps and physics extras name their idea too, so a measured
  voltage in week 3 is retrieved as a calculation in week 5.
- **Evidence, not points.** The skills map levels from what actually happened: attempts, logs,
  recordings, and dated notes a parent adds for things outside the app (a chess class, a
  lesson). A level is a sentence a child could say about themselves, not a number.
- **A portfolio that grows on its own.** Photos, code, recordings, written proofs, debate
  transcripts, Maker's Logs and Practice logs, stamped and dated, laid out as a field journal.
- **Honest completion and no guilt.** A quest is done when its artifact and log exist, or every
  answer is checked. Days are never counted. A child who stops mid-week finds everything saved
  and a plain way back.
- **The AI is a practice partner, not an oracle.** With a family's own Anthropic key, the debate
  room fields an opponent and a coach; without one, the debate is held out loud with a grown-up
  and logged the same way. Every hint, explanation and lesson is authored by a person.
- **Knowing what you know.** Before a check the child taps how sure they are; after a first miss
  they say what kind of miss it was (misread, did not know, slipped). The skills map and the
  Parent view turn those marks into two plain sentences, because a child who is sure and wrong
  needs a different fix from one who is guessing and right.
- **Games that teach.** One game a sprint, played with the family and chosen for its payload:
  Set, Mastermind, Dots and boxes, Hex, guess-my-rule, the 24 game, Kerbal Space Program,
  Battleship, the Rubik's cube, Sprouts, Blokus, Quarto. Chess tactics ride the Think track as
  warm-ups from season 2, every position machine-checked.
- **Extras, when there is time.** Each Build week carries bonus tracks and a make-your-own brief
  that never count toward completion.

## What the family sees

- **This Week**: the season strip, the twelve-week trail, the week's cards, the monster
  problem, the season book and the mistake box.
- **A quest**: one step at a time, a rail of the steps, "stuck, come back later" always
  available, and a working space for scratch notes and drawings.
- **Portfolio** and **Skills map** for the child; the Parent view for the grown-up: a weekly
  paragraph in plain words, the mistake box, explain-it answers waiting for a look, logs, the
  per-season shopping list with costs, profiles and their week clocks (which a parent can pause
  and resume, so a break never costs a week), the Play toggle, resets
  that are logged and cannot be undone, and the family's AI key.

## What a family needs

A laptop with Chrome, a phone that can record, and a season's kit: the first season is about
$93 (a micro:bit v2, a breadboard starter kit, a small robot the family may already own, paper
and pencils; a multimeter and a tape measure are optional and only for the physics extras).
Each season lists exactly what it needs and when, in the Parent view. An instrument for Play is
whatever the child already plays.

## Privacy and cost

Everything a child makes stays in the family's own Firebase project. There are no accounts for
children (a child picks a profile; the only sign-in is a parent's Google account), no ads, no
analytics, and nothing leaves the project except, if the family switches it on with its own
key, the debate room's calls to Anthropic. Sign-in is restricted to an allowlist the owner of
the deployment manages by hand, so nobody can sign up on their own. A family's traffic costs
well under a dollar a month on Firebase.

## What is in this repository

- `content/`: the curriculum. Four seasons of Explorer quests
  (`seasons/{1..4}/weeks/NN/{build,think,speak}.json`, plus `play.json` where authored), four
  seasons of Sprout weeks, and the registries (ideas, skills, motions, books, materials). Plain
  JSON, validated by the app's own schema. `docs/content-authoring.md` is the contract every
  file follows, including the rules for hints, ideas, extras, the physics thread and Play.
- `web/`: the app. Next.js 16, React 19, Firebase (Auth, Firestore, Storage). Domain logic is
  pure and tested (`web/lib/domain`), the content is validated and bundled at build time, and
  every screen has a visual regression gate (`npm run verify:ui`).
- `docs/`: the authoring guide, the UI styleguide (an earned checklist: every rule traces to a
  real defect), the deployment guide and the deploy runbook.
- `firestore.rules`, `storage.rules`: one household never sees another; a family's AI key is
  readable by nothing but the server.

## Running it

You need Node 22, the Firebase CLI, and a Firebase project of your own on the Blaze plan (the
hosting function needs it).

```
cd web
npm install
cp .env.local.example .env.development.local   # fill in your Firebase web config
npm run emulators                                # in one terminal
npm run seed                                     # a demo household in the emulator
npm run dev                                      # http://localhost:3000
```

It runs in three ways, described in `docs/DEPLOYMENT.md`: fully local with the Firebase
emulators (one `docker compose up`), on Firebase Hosting with your own project, or as a container
on any host with your own project as the backend.

Sign-in is Google, restricted to an allowlist you manage (`npx tsx scripts/allowlist.ts`). The
first sign-in creates the household after a consent screen; children are added as profiles in the
Parent view. The Anthropic key for the debate room is per family and entered in the Parent view.
`docs/deploy-runbook.md` covers deploying to your own project, including one trap in how
Turbopack externalises firebase-admin.

Gates before a change ships: `npm test`, `npm run test:rules` (emulator rules tests),
`npx tsc --noEmit`, `npx eslint .`, `npm run validate:content`, and `npm run verify:ui` with its
baselines reviewed by eye.

## Adapting the curriculum

Change the JSON, run the validator, and the app follows. Ids are append-only so a child's saved
work keeps pointing at the right thing. The authoring guide describes the pipeline the content
was written with: an author, an adversarial verifier that recomputes every number, a fixer, and
a landing step that never rewrites an id a child has already answered.
